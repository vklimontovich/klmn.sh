There's a guide on startup equity I recommend reading first:
[Team Equity](https://grossmargin.io/articles/team-equity). It's a good overview of how the
different instruments work — RSA, ESOP, RSU — and why they're not interchangeable.

Once you understand the mechanics, something becomes obvious: founders and founding engineers
are not in the same boat. The downside is roughly the same. The upside is not.

## The salary is roughly the same

A seed-stage founder pays themselves about $130–150K, though $200–250K is not uncommon either
once a real round closes. A mid-level engineer at the same company makes $100–145K in the
Bay Area. A senior one makes $180–235K. So if you join as a founding engineer, you're not taking
a meaningful pay cut relative to the founder. 

The risk of failure is also the same. You both spent years on something that returned zero.

So far, symmetric.

## The equity is not

A founder gets the best equity instrument that exists. Restricted stock, granted at near-zero
strike price, with the QSBS clock starting on day one. Five years later, the first $15M of gains
are federal-tax-free. Anything above that is long-term capital gains, ~20%.

A founding engineer joining after the seed round gets none of this. The options are:

- **RSA**, but now the shares have a real FMV. Either you pay for them out of pocket (tens or
  hundreds of thousands), or the company "gifts" them and you get a tax bill on the spread at
  ordinary income rates. Neither is realistic.
- **ESOP** (ISOs or NSOs). The QSBS clock doesn't start until you exercise — and to start it,
  you have to actually write the check and buy the shares, which can be a sizeable amount of
  money for illiquid stock. If you leave the company before exit, you have 90 days to exercise
  or lose the options entirely.
- **RSUs**. Taxed as ordinary income on vesting (or on the double trigger). No QSBS. Federal
  rates near 40%, plus state. Half of your equity gain disappears to taxes.

## A typical example

Two co-founders incorporate a company. Each gets 5,000,000 shares of restricted stock at
$0.0001/share — $500 each. They file 83(b). The QSBS clock starts.

**Seed, month 6.** They raise $4M at $24M post-money. Investors take ~17%. A 10% ESOP pool is carved out pre-money. Founders end with ~36% each.

**Series A, year 2.** They raise $15M at $60M post-money. Investors take 25%. ESOP refreshed
back to 10% post-money. Founders end with ~25% each.

The first founding engineer is hired right after the Series A. They get a 1% ISO grant —
250,000 shares at a $0.50 strike (the 409A FMV). Cost to exercise the full grant: $125K, for
illiquid stock. They don't early-exercise. 4-year vesting, 1-year cliff.

By this point the founder has been holding restricted stock for two years. The QSBS clock is
running. Three more years and the first $15M of their gains is federal-tax-free.

The engineer's QSBS clock hasn't started. It can't, until they exercise. And now they face a
choice that the founder never has to make.

**Path 1: they leave.** Maybe at year 3, vested at 50%. 125K shares of ISOs. They have 90 days
to exercise or lose everything. Exercise cost: $62,500 out of pocket. On top of that, AMT on
the spread between strike and current FMV — if the company has raised a Series B in the
meantime, the FMV could be $3–4/share, so AMT on $400–500K of paper gain, easily another
$100K of tax due that year. Total cash required to keep the equity alive: $150–200K. For
shares that may still never become liquid.

The other option is to walk away from the equity entirely. Many people do.

**Path 2: they stay.** The typical hold from Series A to exit is 7–10 years. Tender offers,
when they happen at all, usually arrive at Series C or D — year 5 or 6 — and let employees
sell a small fraction. The rest stays locked up until the company exits or dies.

So the engineer's equity question is really: are you willing to commit the next 7–10 years of
your career to this one company, on the chance that it exits and on the chance that the exit
is large enough to matter after the tax haircut? Because the alternative is writing a check
the day you leave for paper that may never become real.

Meanwhile the founder's QSBS clock turned five at year 5 either way.

## It's a VC bet, but worse

Joining a startup as an engineer is a VC investment where you put in time instead of money.
Multi-year time, at a salary discount that could have been earned in big tech, in exchange for
a lottery ticket with worse tax treatment than the founder's.

VC works because of the law of large numbers. A fund invests in 30 companies, expects 25 to
fail, and one or two to return the whole fund. You can't do that with your career. You get
maybe two or three founding-engineer bets across your working life. The variance doesn't
average out.

For a skilled engineer with options, big tech is the better expected-value play. The salary is
a multiple higher. RSUs vest on a schedule and are liquid. There's no exercise problem, no
QSBS clock, no acquisition that turns your options into a tax bill. The only reason to take
the founding-engineer seat instead is experience — building from zero, owning a whole system,
the optionality of becoming a founder yourself later. Those are real. But they are not
monetary upside. Calling them monetary upside is the trick.

The exception is when you're 100% sure the company will be exceptional. But "100% sure" is
not a thing in startups. The founders themselves aren't 100% sure.

## What we can do about it

A few things would actually help.

**Extended exercise windows.** A growing minority of companies (Pinterest, Quora, others) give
employees 5–10 years post-departure to exercise instead of 90 days. This is a partial fix —
QSBS still doesn't apply — but it breaks the worst dilemma in ESOP: leave and write a six-figure
check on the way out, or stay locked into the company for years to avoid it.

**Larger grants to compensate.** If the instrument is going to take a 50% haircut, the grant
size should reflect that. A 2% grant treated as ordinary income is equivalent, after tax, to
a ~1% grant of QSBS-eligible restricted stock. Founders and boards should price this honestly.

**Tax reform.** Under §1202, the QSBS clock starts when you actually acquire the stock, and
for an option that means exercise. That's not really ambiguous — it's what the IRS treats as
the date of issuance, and it's what people file. The real problem is that exercising is
expensive. For a founder, "acquiring the stock" means writing a $500 check at incorporation
when the FMV equals the strike. For an engineer joining at Series A, the same act means
writing a six-figure check and paying AMT on the spread between strike and current FMV. So
the clock-start rule looks fair on paper and is brutal in practice — only people who can
afford to exercise early get the QSBS benefit.

The targeted reform isn't changing when the clock starts. It's making early exercise feasible:
allow ISO grants to be treated as QSBS-qualifying from the grant date if the holder files an
83(b)-equivalent election, without requiring the upfront cash and AMT. That would let
engineers start the clock the same day the founder did, at no real cost to the founder.

None of this fully equalizes things. The founder still gets the best deal. But the gap could
be a lot smaller than it is, and the people who could close it are the founders themselves.
